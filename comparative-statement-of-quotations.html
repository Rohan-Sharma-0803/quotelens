What is a comparative statement of quotations?

When you ask three or more suppliers to quote for the same items, their quotes arrive in different layouts: one is a PDF, one is an Excel sheet, one is a photo of a printed letterhead. Some include GST, some say "GST extra", some add freight at the bottom. A comparative statement (also called a quotation comparison sheet or price comparison chart) rewrites all of them into one common format, item by item, so they can be compared fairly.

It is used by purchase departments, contractors, schools, hospitals, housing societies and government offices, and is usually attached to the purchase file as proof that the order was placed on a fair basis.

Format: what columns to include

  1. Item details: serial number, item description, specification or make, quantity and unit. Use the quantity from your enquiry, not each supplier's quote.
  2. For each supplier: rate per unit (excluding GST) and amount (quantity × rate).
  3. Lowest rate per item and which supplier offered it.
  4. Below the items: basic total, freight/delivery, loading or installation, other charges, taxable value, GST % and GST amount, and the grand total (landed cost).
  5. Ranking: L1, L2, L3 on the grand total.
  6. Commercial terms: payment terms, delivery period, quote validity, warranty, and whether the offer meets the specification.
  7. Recommendation and signatures: prepared by, checked by, approved by.

How to prepare it, step by step

  1. Send the same enquiry to every supplier with the same item list, quantities, delivery location and required terms. Different enquiries give quotes you cannot compare.
  2. Check each quote against the specification before comparing prices. A cheaper item that does not meet the specification is not a valid offer.
  3. Bring every rate to the same basis. If one supplier quotes GST-inclusive rates, divide by 1.18 (for 18% GST) to get the rate excluding GST.
  4. Fill the item rows: amount = your quantity × the supplier's rate. Leave a cell blank (not zero) if a supplier did not quote that item, and note it.
  5. Add every charge mentioned anywhere in the quote: freight, packing, loading, installation, insurance. Read the terms at the bottom carefully; "transport extra" means you must ask for the amount.
  6. Add GST on the taxable value (basic total + charges) and calculate the grand total for each supplier.
  7. Rank L1, L2, L3 on the grand total, then write a short recommendation. If you do not pick L1, give the reason.

Worked example: three quotations for office furniture

An office needs 10 chairs, 10 desks and 4 storage cabinets and gets three quotes:

  • Vendor A: chair ₹6,200, desk ₹8,400, cabinet ₹11,500. Free delivery. GST 18% extra. 30 days credit.
  • Vendor B: chair ₹5,900, desk ₹8,900, cabinet ₹10,800. Freight ₹3,500. GST 18% extra. 50% advance.
  • Vendor C: chair ₹7,198, desk ₹9,912, cabinet ₹13,216, all inclusive of GST. Installation ₹2,000 + GST. Payment against delivery.

Vendor C's rates look the highest, but they include GST. Dividing by 1.18 gives ₹6,100, ₹8,400 and ₹11,200 excluding GST. Now everything is on the same basis:

Common mistakes

  • Comparing GST-inclusive rates with GST-extra rates. An 18% difference hidden in one column decides the result.
  • Ignoring charges in the fine print such as "freight extra", "loading/unloading extra", "installation @ ₹18 per sq ft" or "ex-godown".
  • Comparing different quantities. If a supplier quoted 8 instead of 10, recalculate on your quantity.
  • Treating a missing item as zero. A quote that leaves out an item looks cheaper; price the gap from another supplier before ranking.
  • Different specifications or brands in the same row (for example 12 mm vs 12.5 mm board, or different wattage).
  • Expired quotes. Check validity; a 7-day quote may need to be re-confirmed.
  • Arithmetic slips when copying numbers by hand. Check that quantity × rate matches each supplier's own line total.
Tip: keep the original quotations attached to the statement. Auditors and approvers will check the rates against them.

Frequently asked questions

What is a comparative statement of quotations?

It is a table that puts the quotations from different suppliers for the same requirement side by side, item by item, with their rates, amounts, charges, taxes and terms, so you can see which supplier offers the lowest total cost and which one to recommend.

Who prepares the comparative statement?

Usually the purchase or procurement team, or whoever collected the quotations. It is then checked and approved by someone else, such as the department head or the finance team, before the purchase order is placed.

What does L1, L2 and L3 mean?

L1 is the supplier with the lowest total cost, L2 the second lowest and L3 the third. The ranking should be done on the landed cost: basic price plus freight, other charges and GST, after confirming every quote meets the specification.

Should GST be included when comparing quotations?

Compare on the same basis. Either convert every quote to rates excluding GST and add GST once at the end, or compare all-inclusive totals. Mixing GST-inclusive and GST-extra rates in the same row is the most common mistake.

Is the lowest quotation always selected?

Not always. The L1 supplier is normally preferred, but a slightly higher quote can be justified by better payment terms, faster delivery, longer warranty or a better technical fit. Write the reason in the recommendation.
